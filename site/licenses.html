Licensing guide

Licenses, in plain words

Every project on Brainchild needs a license. Without one, nobody can legally continue it. This page explains the common ones and how Brainchild records them.

Code licenses

Brainchild reads the license file in your repository. Any license file is accepted; one GitHub doesn't recognise is shown as "Custom license". These are the common ones.

MIT
Do almost anything with the code, including selling it or taking it closed-source. Just keep the copyright notice and license with it. Credit required · No patent grant · Doesn't state it's irrevocable
Apache-2.0
Like MIT, plus a patent grant from the people who contributed. Changed files must say they were changed, and any NOTICE file is kept. It gives no right to use the project's name or trademarks. Credit required · Patent grant · States it's irrevocable
GPL-3.0 and GPL-2.0
Anyone may use, change and share the code, but a copy they share, changed or not, must stay under the same license with its source available. Credit required · Copies stay GPL
LGPL-2.1 and LGPL-3.0
Like the GPL for the library itself, but other programs may use the library without becoming GPL. Changes to the library stay LGPL
AGPL-3.0
Like the GPL, and it also applies when people use the program over a network: whoever runs a changed copy as a service must offer its source. Copies stay AGPL

Art, music and other assets

Code licenses are written for code. For art, music and sound, the listing form offers Creative Commons licenses, or "same as the code".

CC BY 4.0
Use, change and share the assets for any purpose, as long as you credit the creator. This is the one we recommend. Credit required · Commercial use allowed · States it's irrevocable
CC BY-SA 4.0
Like CC BY, but changed versions must be shared under CC BY-SA too. Credit required · Changes stay CC BY-SA
CC0
The creator gives up their rights as far as the law allows: anyone can use the assets for anything, with no credit needed.
CC BY-NC 4.0
Use, change and share the assets with credit, but not to make money: nobody may sell the project or its art. Credit required · Non-commercial
CC BY-NC-SA 4.0
Like CC BY-NC, and changed art must be shared under CC BY-NC-SA too. Credit required · Non-commercial · Changes stay CC BY-NC-SA

Non-commercial art is allowed because Brainchild is free and non-commercial anyway, and many makers don't want someone selling their game. The project can still be adopted and continued; it just can't be sold. Projects with non-commercial art carry a "Non-commercial art" label, and Browse has a "Commercial use allowed" filter for people who want a project they could sell one day.

  • "Commercial" can be a grey area: a monetised video or stream, a "pay what you want" page or a Patreon may count. If in doubt, ask the maker.
  • Mixing: CC BY-NC-SA art can't be combined with CC BY-SA art into one changed piece, because each license requires the result to use its own. Separate files under CC BY or CC0 can sit alongside it.
  • No "no derivatives" (ND) licenses. Art that nobody may change stops a project being continued, so CC BY-ND and CC BY-NC-ND aren't accepted.

How Brainchild records a license

  • Each listing, handoff and adoption records the license at that exact commit, with a fingerprint of the license text and a stored copy. You can see the record from a project's page.
  • Earlier releases can't be withdrawn. If a maker changes the license later, copies made under the earlier license keep the rights it gave. Records are only ever added, never changed.
  • Permissive code can be relicensed. Someone who adopts MIT or Apache-2.0 code may put their copy under another license, as long as they keep the original notices. Brainchild records it as a new version.
  • GPL stays GPL. A project under a GPL-family license (GPL, LGPL or AGPL) keeps it when it changes hands. Brainchild refuses an adoption whose copy has a different license.
  • The project's name is separate from its code. Apache-2.0 says outright that it gives no right to a name or trademark, and the other licenses don't give one either. When you hand a project off, the listing asks whether the next maintainer may keep the name, call theirs a continuation, or should rename it.

Why does my license show as Custom?

GitHub and GitLab name a license only when the LICENSE file is its standard text. When they can't, Brainchild looks for the full text of a standard license inside the file itself.

  • The usual cause is extra text inside LICENSE: a note about which folders it covers, a second license for the art, or changed wording. Sometimes the file just isn't a standard license.
  • The fix: keep LICENSE as the plain, unchanged text of one standard license, and put notes about what it covers in your README. Then press Check again on the listing page, or save the project's Edit page.
  • Different licenses for code and art? Put the code license in LICENSE and the art license in a separate file, for example LICENSE-ART.md, and choose the art license in the listing's license step.
  • If you keep the extra wording, Brainchild shows it to you and asks whether it only explains what the license covers or adds conditions. If it only explains, the project is listed under the standard license, and the wording is kept on the License record as a note. If it adds conditions, the project is listed as Custom, and the conditions are shown clearly on the project page and License record. A changed license is never treated as the standard one.
  • Standard texts to copy (put your name and the year in the copyright line): MIT, Apache 2.0, GPL 3.0, MPL 2.0, and for art CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0. These are the texts from choosealicense.com.

Brainchild never gets write access to your repository, so it can't change the file for you.

Mods and plugins

A mod's or plugin's license covers only its maker's own files. It gives no rights to what it's made for (a game, a browser, an editor), which keeps its own license and terms. Every mod and plugin on Brainchild says what it's made for, so people can check what they need.

AI-generated parts

Brainchild asks makers to list which parts were generated, with which tool, in the handoff packet and in CREDITS.md. Whether and how AI-generated material can be protected differs from country to country, so say plainly what was made by hand.

This guide is a plain summary to help you choose. It isn't legal advice. The license text in each repository is what counts; if something matters, read it or ask a lawyer.
